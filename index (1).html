Introduction

Steel surface defects broadly fall into two physical categories. Scratch defects are structural, linear anomalies caused by mechanical abrasion during rolling, cutting, or handling — manifesting as bright linear features under directional illumination. Patch defects are textural, stochastic anomalies caused by material inclusions, pitting corrosion, or surface oxidation, appearing as irregular dark or bright regions without dominant linear geometry.

A critical practical challenge is the domain gap between the NEU Surface Defect Dataset (laboratory-controlled images) and real-world factory or web-sourced images. Our Standardization Pipeline using Histogram Equalization resolves this by forcing every image to the same mathematical intensity manifold, regardless of source.

Problem Statement & Scope

Steel surface images from different sources have inconsistent lighting, resolutions, and noise levels. Classical models trained on one domain frequently fail on the other. This project develops a domain-robust, dual-branch classical CV binary classifier suitable for MSME environments.

IN — Input

Standard RGB Images

Steel surface images of arbitrary resolution from any camera. No specialized imaging hardware required.

OUT — Output

Binary Classification

Scratch (label 0) or Patch (label 1) — with per-class confidence scores via Streamlit interface.

CON — Constraints

Classical CV Only

No deep learning. CPU-based inference at 30+ FPS. Requires only ~500 labeled samples to generalize reliably.

TGT — Target Users

MSME Workshops

Metal workshops in Punjab, Haryana, Rajasthan producing steel components for automotive and agricultural markets.

Research Gaps Addressed

Three significant gaps in the existing literature motivated the key design decisions in this project.

Gap 1 — Domain Gap in Surface Defect Datasets

Most published systems are trained and evaluated on a single controlled dataset such as NEU or DAGM. When applied to images from different lighting or cameras, performance degrades significantly. Classical CV approaches have not systematically addressed this [He et al., 2019].

Gap 2 — Monolithic Feature Vectors for Structurally Distinct Defects

Existing handcrafted approaches concatenate all feature types into a single vector. The deliberate architectural separation of geometric and textural branches to match the physical nature of different defect types has not been widely studied [Xie, 2008].

Gap 3 — Selective PCA on Texture Branch Only

Most works apply PCA globally. The Hough line count is a scalar with direct physical interpretation — compressing it with PCA alongside texture features would corrupt its geometric meaning. Selective PCA on the LBP branch alone is an underexplored design pattern [Ojala et al., 2002].

§4

Literature Review

Key papers reviewed during development. Gap identified: limited work targets affordable, explainable, lightweight screening systems for small-scale metal manufacturing with cross-domain robustness.

LBP (Ojala et al., 2002)

Uniform LBP provides compact, rotation-invariant texture descriptors — the basis of Branch B micro-texture analysis.

SVM (Cortes & Vapnik, 1995)

Maximum-margin classifier — well-established for high-confidence binary decisions on compact, well-engineered feature vectors.

Canny (Canny, 1986)

Optimal multi-stage edge detector with thin, connected edges — forms the input to the Hough Transform in Branch A.

Hough Transform (Duda & Hart, 1972)

Voting-based line detection in polar parameter space — provides the scalar geometric feature for linear scratch detection.

Surface Defect Survey (Xie, 2008)

Texture-based methods remain competitive on structured industrial datasets; motivates our LBP-PCA-SVM pipeline design.
